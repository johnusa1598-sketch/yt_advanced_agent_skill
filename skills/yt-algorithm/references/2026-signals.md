# 2026 signals — research notes, 26 October 2026

Read this with `yt-algorithm/SKILL.md`. Everything here is a
third-party report or a study, NOT an official YouTube threshold.
YouTube does not publish its ranking formula. Use every range
below only as orientation, and judge a video against its own
channel's median in the same format, never against these ranges.

## Shorts feed: the first test is the swipe

In the Shorts feed there is no thumbnail click, so the hook IS
the click. Studio's "viewed vs swiped away" (VVSA) rate is the
first gate:

- One 2025 analysis of 3.3 billion Shorts, reported by a
  third-party guide, puts the healthy VVSA range at roughly
  70–90%. Below about 60% it reports distribution collapsing.
  Treat those as reported ranges, not YouTube rules.
- A separate Shorts study reports a strong link between the
  first 3 seconds and total retention (correlation 0.72):
  holding about 80% through the first 3 seconds typically
  goes with 65–75% total retention; holding only 30% goes
  with 20–35%.
- The practical rule does not need the exact numbers: if the
  first-3-seconds leak is the biggest drop, fix the hook, not
  the body. If VVSA is healthy but completion is low, the
  promise or the body is the problem (`/yt-diagnose` reads
  the gates in this order).

## Five hook patterns that stop the swipe

Reported across several 2026 Shorts guides, in order of
usefulness for a visual skill channel:

1. Visual disruption — a sharp cut, zoom, or text overlay on
   frame one. The brain registers change and pauses the swipe.
2. Face and voice — a face speaking to camera. Brains
   prioritise faces and speech, buying 2–3 seconds.
3. Unexpected visual — a fail, a contradiction, something
   visibly wrong. Curiosity delays the swipe.
4. Rapid scene change or music beat in the first 1–2 seconds.
   Momentum makes the next beat feel worth waiting for.
5. Text-driven curiosity — a number, a benefit, or a question
   on frame one ("Rate it 1–10", "Did she land it?").

A working hook formula, from the same guides: statement in
the first 3 seconds, then a proof point (a specific number
or result), then a promise of what watching to the end gives.
All three fit in 8–12 seconds total. No greeting, no logo,
no setup before the statement.

## Pacing, length, captions, loops

- Visual change every 2–4 seconds: a cut, zoom, text pop, or
  angle switch. A static frame for 10+ seconds is reported
  as a swipe trigger.
- Length, third-party sweet spots, not rules: 20–35 seconds
  is the most-cited range for 2026; under 15 seconds gives
  the system little data to separate a strong Short from an
  accidental swipe-through; over 45 seconds the retention
  maths gets much harder. For a visual skill, 18–25 seconds
  for a single trick and 35–55 for a short narrative arc.
- Captions: over 60% of vertical video is watched muted or
  at low volume in one guide's claim, and karaoke-style
  captions are reported at 30–40% higher completion than
  uncaptioned. Captions must reinforce the hook, not repeat
  the spoken line word for word.
- Loop: a loop works when the final frame visually continues
  the opening frame with no cognitive break — same motion,
  same palette, or a question the opening answers and the
  ending re-asks. In Studio a working loop shows as a
  retention spike above 100% at second 1. Completion above
  about 85% is associated, in third-party guides, with
  breakout Shorts.
- Call to action: place the single CTA in the payoff or
  immediately after it, never as a long end-card. Multiple
  CTAs ("like, comment, subscribe, follow") are reported
  as overwhelming; pick one, and put harder asks in the
  pinned comment.

## Satisfaction signals, stated carefully

As in `SKILL.md`, no official hierarchy change is claimed
here. The signals third-party 2026 guides layer on top of
retention are: post-watch surveys, repeat viewing, session
continuation (did the viewer watch more YouTube after this
video), shares, and comment sentiment. The Studio check is
the same either way: read all five against your own median,
and a video that starts or extends a session is worth more
than a video with high watch time but a high exit rate.

## CTR and retention: orientation ranges only

Sources disagree with each other, which is itself the point:
do not chase one number.

- Overall CTR: most channels roughly 2–10%; half of all
  channels fall in that band per YouTube's own statement as
  reported by several guides.
- By traffic source (third-party guides, 2025–2026):
  Browse/Home roughly 2–5%, subscribers 4–8% (strong 8–20%),
  Suggested 3–5% (strong 7–12%), Search 8–15%. A 4% Browse
  CTR can be healthy; a 4% Search CTR for a targeted query
  deserves investigation.
- By length, average retention (third-party guides):
  under 5 minutes roughly 50–70%, 5–10 minutes 40–55%,
  10–20 minutes 35–45%, 20+ minutes 25–35%. One guide puts
  the platform average at 23.7% with over 55% leaving in
  the first minute — use it to set expectations, not targets.
- Channels that improve retention by 10 points are reported
  to see 25%+ more impressions. Again: a reported effect,
  not a promise.

## Posting frequency: what the 2026 study actually says

Metricool's 2026 YouTube Study, as reported, finds per-post
views highest at 2–4 Shorts per week, but monthly totals
climbing with volume because the feed gives every upload
another shot. The pack's rule stands: post as often as
quality holds, never at quality's expense, and never from a
generic "best time" table — the earlier research found those
tables self-contradictory. Use the channel's own Audience
heatmap in Studio for timing.

## Shorts thumbnails still matter, just elsewhere

Shorts autoplay in the feed, so the thumbnail rarely gets
a feed click — but it appears on the channel grid, in
search results, and in the related-Shorts panel next to
long-form videos. One guide reports a custom grid-readable
thumbnail lifting cross-format discovery by 15–25%.
Spend five minutes on a thumbnail for a Short you want to
live on the channel page, not for the feed push itself.
